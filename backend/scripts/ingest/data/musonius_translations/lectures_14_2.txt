The beginning of establishing a household is marriage. And so whoever does away with marriage among human beings does away with the household, and does away with the city, and does away with the whole human race. For it could not endure if there were no birth of children, and there would be no birth—at least none just and lawful—if there were no marriage. For it is clear that a household or a city is composed neither of women alone nor of men alone, but of the community of the two with one another; and one could find no other community of men and women that is more necessary or more dear. For what comrade is so agreeable to a comrade as a beloved wife is to her husband? What brother to a brother? What son to his parents? Who, when absent, is so longed for as a husband by his wife and a wife by her husband? Whose presence would more surely lighten grief, or increase joy, or set right misfortune? For whom is it held that all things are held in common—bodies and souls and property—except for a husband and wife? For these reasons all human beings consider the affection between husband and wife to be the oldest of all; and no mother or father in his right mind claims to be dearer to his own child than the one joined to that child in marriage. And that well-known story too seems to make clear how much the affection of a wife toward her husband surpasses that of parents toward their children: for when Admetus received this gift from the gods—that, if he could provide someone to die in his place, he would live twice the time allotted to him—he did not find his parents willing to die before him, though they were already old; but his wedded wife Alcestis, though she was quite young, readily accepted death in place of her husband.
