And indeed it is not impossible for a human being to become such. For we have no other source from which to conceive these virtues than from human nature itself, having met with certain people of this kind, whom, because they were such, men used to call divine and godlike. If, then, someone should happen, while he was still young, to have taken care over a right education, and should have learned in no deficient way all that belongs to the noble branches of learning, and should have trained himself sufficiently in what can be trained, such a man in old age, making use of the resources present within him, would live in accordance with nature; and he would bear without distress the loss of the pleasures of youth, and would be without distress at the present weakness of his body; nor would he be discontented either at being despised by those near him or at being neglected by his household and friends, inasmuch as against all these things he holds within his own mind a fine remedy: the education he possesses. But if someone should have shared in education more deficiently, yet should display eagerness toward the better things and be able to be persuaded by what is well said, such a man would do well to seek to hear words of counsel from those who have made it their work to know what things are harmful to human beings and what beneficial, and in what manner one might escape the former and acquire the latter, and how one might gently receive, when they come upon him, the things which are not in fact evils but only seem to be. And by hearing these things and being persuaded by them (since to hear without being persuaded is most unprofitable) he would pass through his old age well, and among other things he would be rid of the fear of death, which most of all disturbs and oppresses the old, as though they had forgotten that death is owed by every mortal.
