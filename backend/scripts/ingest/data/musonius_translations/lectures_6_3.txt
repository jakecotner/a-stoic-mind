In summary, then, it has been more or less stated what each mode of training is like. Nevertheless, I shall try to say in detail how each thing should be done, no longer separating or distinguishing the exercises common to the soul and the body from those proper to the soul, but going through the exercises of each part in a mixed fashion. Now, since we happen to have heard and accepted these things — all of us, at least, who have shared in the philosopher's teaching — that neither hardship nor death nor poverty is in any way an evil, nor anything else free from vice, and that, again, wealth, life, and pleasure are not good, nor anything else that has no share in virtue; yet, even having accepted these things, on account of the corruption that has taken hold of us straight from childhood, and the wicked habituation produced by that corruption, when hardship approaches us we consider that an evil is approaching, and when pleasure comes to us we consider that a good is arriving, and we shudder at death as the ultimate calamity, while we welcome life as the greatest of goods; and when we give away money we grieve as though we were being harmed, but when we receive it we rejoice as though we were being benefited; and in like manner, in a great many other matters as well, we deal with things not in accordance with the correct judgments, but rather follow our base habit. Since, then, I say that this is how these matters stand, the one who trains must seek to master himself, so as not to delight in pleasure, nor to shrink from hardship; not to love living, nor to fear death; and, in the case of money, not to prefer receiving over giving.
