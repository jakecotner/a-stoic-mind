And the very thing that makes life most wretched for the old is just this: the fear of death — as indeed the orator Isocrates too admitted. For they say that when someone asked him how he was getting on, he replied that he fared as one might expect of a man who was ninety years old and considered death to be the worst of evils. And yet, how could such a man have had any share of education, or of knowledge of things truly good or evil, when he supposed that to be an evil which follows of necessity upon the best life? For if the best life is that of the good man, then the end even of this life is death. But, as I was saying, if someone should secure this in old age — to await death without fear and with confidence — he would have provided himself with no small resource toward living without distress and in accordance with nature. And he would gain this by associating with those who are philosophers not merely in name but in truth, provided that he is also willing to be persuaded by them. I therefore say that the best provision for old age is the one I mentioned at the beginning of the discourse: to live in accordance with nature, doing and thinking what one ought. For in this way the old man would be both most cheerful and most praiseworthy, and, possessing these things, he would live in good fortune and in honor. But if anyone supposes that wealth is the greatest consolation for the old, and that it enables them to live without distress, he supposes wrongly. For wealth is able to provide people with the pleasures that come from food and drink and sex and other such things, but it could in no way provide either good cheer or freedom from distress to the one who possesses it. Witnesses to this are the many wealthy people who are distressed and downhearted and consider themselves wretched. For this reason wealth would not be a fine relief for old age either.
