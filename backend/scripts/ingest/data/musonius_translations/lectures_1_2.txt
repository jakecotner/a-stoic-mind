That these things are so, he said, we would readily understand if we imagined a boy or a young man—the one reared in every kind of luxury, his body made soft and his soul enfeebled by habits that lead toward weakness, and moreover showing himself sluggish and slow to learn by nature; and the other, on the contrary, brought up in a somewhat Spartan manner, not accustomed to luxury, practiced in endurance, and readily attentive to what is rightly said. Then, if we set these two young men to listen to a philosopher speaking about death, about toil, about poverty, and about similar things, that they are not evils, and again about life, about pleasure, about wealth, and about things akin to these, that they are not goods—will both of them alike welcome and be persuaded by what is said? It cannot be affirmed. Rather, the more sluggish one would perhaps scarcely and slowly, and as if pried loose by countless arguments, at last give his assent; while the other will quickly and readily receive what is said, as belonging to him and befitting him, requiring neither many demonstrations nor greater effort.
