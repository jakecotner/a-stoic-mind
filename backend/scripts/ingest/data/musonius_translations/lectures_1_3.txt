Or was that Spartan boy not of this sort, the one who asked Cleanthes the philosopher whether toil is a good? For he clearly was so well endowed by nature and so well reared toward virtue that he considered toil to be nearer to the nature of the good than to that of evil; indeed, taking it as agreed that toil is not an evil, he asked whether it happens to be a good. And so Cleanthes, admiring the boy, said to him: "You are of the blood of a noble race, dear child, such things you speak." How, then, could such a one not be easily persuaded neither to fear poverty nor death nor any other of the things that seem fearsome, and again not to pursue wealth or life or pleasure?

But to return to the beginning of the discussion, I say that the teacher of philosophy must not seek to go through a multitude of arguments and demonstrations before his students, but must speak effectively about each matter and reach the mind of the hearer, and say things that . . . are persuasive and not easily overturned, and above all by presenting himself, in his conduct concerning these things, as one who handles his hearers by this means; while the student must be intent upon what is said, and take care that he does not unawares accept something false, and, by Zeus, not seek to hear many demonstrations of what is true, but clear ones; and whatever of the counsels he is persuaded are his own and true, these he must follow up in his life. For only in this way will anyone be benefited by philosophy, if to the arguments he has accepted, which are sound, he renders his deeds in harmony.
