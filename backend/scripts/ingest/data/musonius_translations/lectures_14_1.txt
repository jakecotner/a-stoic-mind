When someone else said that marriage and life with a wife seemed to him to be an obstacle to the practice of philosophy, Musonius replied: It was no obstacle to Pythagoras, nor to Socrates, nor to Crates, each of whom lived with a wife; and no one could point to others who practiced philosophy better than they did. And yet Crates was without a home, without possessions, and utterly without property, and nevertheless he married; then, having no dwelling of his own, he spent his days and nights in the public colonnades of Athens together with his wife. But we, who set out from a home, and some of us with household slaves to serve us, still dare to say that marriage is an obstacle to philosophy? Now surely the philosopher is the teacher and guide of humankind in all matters that belong to a human being according to nature; and marrying, if anything is, is manifestly according to nature. For why else did the creator of humankind first divide our race in two, and then make it with two sets of genitals, the one female and the other male, and then implant in each a strong desire for the company and union of the other, and blend into both a strong longing for one another—in the male for the female, in the female for the male? Is it not, then, evident that he wished them to be together and to live together and to contrive together the things that pertain to life, and to bring about the birth and rearing of children in common, so that our race might be everlasting? Well then, tell me: is it fitting that each person should attend also to his neighbor's affairs, and see to it that there are households in his city, and that the city not be left desolate, and that the common good be well maintained? For if you say that one should look only to his own concerns, you declare that a human being is no different from a wolf or any of the other most savage beasts, whose nature it is to live by violence and greed, sparing nothing from which they may reap some pleasure, having no share in community, no share in the cooperation that comes from one another, no share in justice of any kind. But if you will agree that human nature closely resembles that of the bee—which cannot live alone, for it perishes when isolated, but bends its effort toward one common work of its kind, and joins in acting and working together with those near it—if this is so, and if in addition it is held that vice in a human being consists in injustice and savagery and indifference toward the misfortune of one's neighbor, while virtue is love of humankind, kindness, justice, being a benefactor, and being caring toward one's neighbor—then in this way each person must take thought for his own city, and must build a household as a bulwark for the city.
