Would that man, one may say, be quickly provoked at being reviled among a few, when even being reviled in the theater did not make him indignant? And Phocion the good, when his wife was insulted by someone, was so far from bringing a charge against the one who had insulted her that, when the man in fear approached and asked Phocion to forgive him, saying that he had not known she was that man's wife against whom he had offended—Phocion said: "But my wife has suffered nothing at your hands; some other woman perhaps has. So you need not even make any defense to me." And I could name many other men as well who have experienced outrage, some wronged by certain people with the tongue, others with the hands so that their bodies were maltreated; and they are seen neither to have retaliated against those who wronged them, nor to have proceeded against them in any other way, but to have borne their injustice very gently. For indeed to consider how one may bite back the one who has bitten, and how one may return the harm to the one who began it, belongs to some wild beast rather than to a man, since such a creature cannot even reckon this: that most of the wrongs committed by people are committed through ignorance and lack of learning, from which a man, once taught otherwise, at once desists. To receive people's wrongs, however, not savagely, and not to be implacable toward those who have offended, but rather to be to them a cause of good hope, belongs to a gentle and humane character. How much better it is, then, for the philosopher to appear so disposed that he thinks it right to grant pardon if someone has offended against him, than to seem to be defending himself by going to law and bringing charges, while in truth behaving in an unseemly way, doing things inconsistent with his own words.
