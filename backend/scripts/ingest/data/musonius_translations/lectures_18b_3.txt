But surely, if God had contrived food for us for the sake of pleasure, we ought to feel pleasure from it during this longer period, and not only in that briefest time in which we swallow. Yet nevertheless, for the sake of that briefest time in which we feel pleasure, an endless preparation of delicacies goes on, the sea is sailed to its farthest limits, cooks are more eagerly sought after than farmers, and some set out dinners that cost the price of estates—and this though the body is in no way benefited by the extravagance of the foods. For quite the contrary, those who use the cheapest foods are the strongest. At any rate, you would see that, for the most part, servants are stronger than their masters, country folk than city dwellers, and the poor than the rich, and that they are more capable of hard work, less exhausted by their labors, more rarely ill, and more easily able to endure cold, heat, sleeplessness, and every such thing. But even if extravagant and cheap food were equally to strengthen the body, still one should choose the cheap, because it is more conducive to self-control and more befitting a good man; likewise, that which is easily obtained is more fitting for decent people than what is hard to obtain, that which is free of trouble than what involves trouble, and that which is ready at hand than what is not ready for nourishment. And, to state in summary the whole matter concerning food, I say that one must make its aim health and strength, since one should eat only for the sake of these, which require no extravagance at all; and that in eating one must attend to good order and the fitting measure, and above all to being clean and unhurried.
