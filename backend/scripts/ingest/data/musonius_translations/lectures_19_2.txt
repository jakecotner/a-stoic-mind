From this one can also see that the day-runners do not use footwear on their journeys, and that among athletes the runners would not be able to maintain their speed if they had to run in shoes. And since it is for the sake of shelter that we also build our houses, I say that these too should be built with a view to what is necessary for use—so as to keep off cold, and to keep off the severity of heat, and to be a defense against sun and winds for those who need it. In general, whatever a natural cave might provide, one that offers a person a moderate place to enter, this is what our house ought to provide us, being at most only so much more elaborate as to have a suitable place for storing human food. But what of courtyards surrounded by columns? What of the many-colored plasterwork? What of ceilings roofed with gold? What of the extravagance of stones, some fitted together on the floor, others set into the walls, some even brought from very far away and at very great expense? Are not all these things superfluous and unnecessary—things without which it is possible both to live and to be healthy—yet which involve the greatest trouble and come about only through much money, from which one could have benefited many people both publicly and privately? And indeed, how much more glorious it is to benefit many people than to live extravagantly! How much more noble it is to spend on human beings than to spend on wood and stones! How much more useful it is to possess many friends than to be surrounded by a great house—which is what results for the one who eagerly does good! What could anyone gain from the greatness and beauty of a house so great as what he gains from doing kindness to his city and his fellow citizens from his own resources?
