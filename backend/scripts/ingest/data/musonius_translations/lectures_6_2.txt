How, then, and in what manner should these people train? Since it happens that a human being is neither soul alone nor body alone, but something composite made from these two, the one who trains must attend to both — more to the better part, as is fitting, that is, to the soul; but also to the other, if indeed no part of the human being is to be left deficient. For the body of the one who pursues philosophy must also be well prepared for the works of the body, since often the virtues make use of it as a necessary instrument for the actions of life. Of training, then, one kind would rightly be proper to the soul alone, while another is common to both the soul and the body. Now training common to both will come about when we accustom ourselves to cold and heat, to thirst and hunger, to plainness of food and hardness of bed, to abstinence from pleasant things and endurance of laborious ones. For through these and things like them the body is strengthened and becomes resistant to hardship, sturdy, and useful for every task; and the soul too is strengthened, being trained through the endurance of laborious things toward courage, and through abstinence from pleasant things toward self-control. And the training proper to the soul is, first, to keep at hand the demonstrations concerning the things that seem good — that they are not good — and those concerning the things that seem bad — that they are not bad — and to become accustomed to recognizing the things that are truly good and to distinguishing them from those that are not truly good. Then, to practice neither fleeing anything that seems bad nor pursuing anything that seems good, but to turn away by every device from the things that are truly bad, and to pursue by every means the things that are truly good.
