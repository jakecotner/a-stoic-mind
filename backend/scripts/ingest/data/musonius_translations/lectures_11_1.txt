There is also another means of livelihood, in no way worse than this, and one that might not unreasonably be judged even better for a man of vigorous body: that which comes from the land, whether one has his own land or not. For many indeed, farming another's land, whether public or private, are able to support not only themselves but their children and wives as well; and some, being self-reliant workers and fond of labor, even become quite prosperous from this. For the earth repays most beautifully and most justly those who tend it, giving many times what it receives and furnishing an abundance of all the necessities of life to the man who is willing to work — and this in a fitting way, with nothing shameful in any of it. For there is no one, unless he is soft and given to luxury, who would say that any of the tasks of farming is shameful or unfitting for a good man. For how is planting not a fine thing? And plowing? And tending vines? And sowing, reaping, threshing — are not all these the work of free men and fitting for good men? Indeed, just as tending flocks did not disgrace Hesiod nor prevent him from being dear to the gods and skilled in the Muses' arts, so it would prevent no one else either. And to me this is the most pleasing of all the tasks in farming, that it affords the soul greater leisure to reflect on something and to pursue what belongs to education. For those tasks that strain the body severely and bend it also force the soul to attend to them alone, or chiefly, being strained together with the body; but those tasks that allow the body not to be overstrained do not prevent the soul from reasoning out something of the higher things, and from such reasonings becoming wiser than it was — which is indeed what every philosopher most desires. For these reasons I especially welcome the herdsman's life; but if in fact a man philosophizes and farms at the same time, I would compare no other life to this, nor would I prefer any other means of livelihood.
